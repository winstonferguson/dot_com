# Tone of voice

Speak like an operator who has run and turned around businesses, talking to someone running one under pressure now: plain, specific and honest, never like a vendor or a consultant selling a method.

The reader is the owner or general manager of an owner-run UK business under pressure: cashflow, costs or growth that the business can't keep up with. They are busy, not technical, and wary of AI hype and of anyone selling them software. They decide in seconds whether a page is about them, so the copy has to sound like someone who has run a business through that, not someone pitching to people who have.

The offer is an operator-led, fixed-price review. Money and operations come first: cashflow, the financial model, where to focus. Then the systems that fix what the review finds. Software is a tool to reach for, not the pitch.

## Five principles

| Principle | Do | Don't | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Say what you do, plainly | Open on who you are and what you do: "I've run and turned around businesses, and now I build the software that keeps them running." Let the reader recognise their own problem in the detail below. | Opening on what the reader is missing or doing wrong, or "I help SMEs unlock digital transformation." | A plain statement reads as confident; a pain-led opener reads as a pitch. |
| Be specific | Name the task, the tool, the number: "a ranked list of fixes", "the invoice spreadsheet". | "A comprehensive, tailored solution." | Specific nouns prove you know the work. |
| Use everyday business words | Cashflow, debts, costs, job, order, invoice, rota, spreadsheet, re-typing, chasing. | Consultant words: stakeholders, workflows, processes, synergies. | Their words, not a consultant's, show you've done this work. |
| Stay calm and honest | Say when software isn't the answer, and what you don't know. | Promise transformation or hype AI. | One overclaim discredits everything around it. |
| Write to one person | "I", "you", short sentences, direct questions. | "We", "our team", long abstract sentences. | You are one person; pretending otherwise reads as a sales front. |

## Don't assert how they work

Headlines say what you do. Below the headline, describe what you look for ("where a job gets typed again, checked twice or waits on someone") or ask, rather than stating how the reader's business works.

- Yes: "How many times does an order get typed before it's invoiced?"
- No: "Your team re-types every order three times." (states their setup as fact, with an invented number)

Ask one genuine question, then say what you do. Avoid questions that answer themselves and rush to a pitch: "Tired of…?", "What if you could…?", "Are you X? Then…".

## Evidence

- Use numbers only when they come from real work: a client's figure, a measured time, a count you did.
- Never invent or round up results ("saves 10 hours a week", "up to 40%").
- Where a figure is missing, leave a marked placeholder such as `[result: TBC]` and tell the user.
- No testimonials or client names unless the user supplies them and confirms permission.

## Words to use and avoid

| Use | Avoid | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| admin, paperwork | back-office operations, business processes | What owners call it. |
| job, order, invoice, rota | project, deliverable, resource allocation | Matches how they talk. |
| spreadsheet, the system you use now | legacy systems, tech stack | Names the actual thing. |
| re-typing, copying across, chasing | data silos, manual processes, inefficiencies | Concrete, visible pain. |
| fix, tool | solution, platform | Plain nouns. |
| owner-run business, growing business | small business, SME, organisation, any size claim | Owners recognise themselves in it; "small business" gets skimmed past. |
| AI, only where it earns its place | revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, AI-powered | Sceptical buyers tune out hype. |
| review, report | audit, teardown, strategy, roadmap, engagement | Plain, and says what happens next. |

Also never: seamless, effortless, transform, unlock, supercharge, empower, leverage, streamline, uniquely positioned, multidisciplinary. If a word could describe any consultant, it probably doesn't belong.

## How the voice flexes

The voice stays the same; the dial moves with the setting.

| Where | Dial | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homepage | Calm, shortest | What you do first, one button. No history of you above the fold. |
| Case studies | Most factual | Problem, what I built, result. Numbers only per "Evidence". No adjectives. |
| Track record | Most factual | Named work, dates, what I did, the result. Every name needs permission; every missing figure gets a visible TODO. |
| About paragraph | Personal, still plain | The owner-operator-who-moved-into-software story, facts only: Roche Bobois, the restaurant, London hospitality, software, Saw & Sew and Datafurn. |
| Outreach email or message | Casual, direct | Two to four lines, one question about a task that eats their week. No pitch. |
| Proposal | Clearest, most structured | Scope, deliverables, price, next step. Plain terms, no jargon. |
| Posts or articles | Practical | Walk-through style: here is how an order gets invoiced, here is where the hours go. |

## Positioning decisions

- **Umbrella:** operator-led business reviews for owner-run UK businesses under pressure. The headline says what you've done and what you do, not what the reader is missing.
- **Lead identity:** an operator who has run and turned around businesses and now builds software, not an engineer who understands business.
- **The offer:** the fixed-price review of money and operations leads; building systems follows. No "free" offer in the CTA.
- **Scope of advice:** operational and financial-modelling advice only. Never present it as insolvency, legal or tax advice. Say what isn't covered.
- **Formality:** first name, friendly and direct.
- **AI:** never in a headline and never its own section. One sentence where it is part of a fix or explains how a fix gets built.
- **Price:** not shown on the site.
- **No "free" offers and no extra sections.** The homepage is: eyebrow, H1, sub-line, one button, What I do, Track record, About, case studies.
- **One ask per piece:** one button on a page, one question in a message.
- **Segment word:** "owner-run businesses" on the site, with no size claim. It lives in `src/_data/site_metadata.yml` (`segment`); pages read it from there, so don't hard-code it in copy.
- **Internal target (never in copy):** 5–50 staff, with an owner or general manager who makes the decision. Use it to filter contact lists, not to describe the reader.
- **Outreach:** name the sector ("restaurant groups", "trade suppliers"), not the size.
- **Referrers:** investors, accountants, landlords and lenders see struggling owner-run businesses first. Copy for them asks for an introduction, not a sale.

## House style

- **Spelling:** British English: specialise, organise, colour, programme. Correct American spellings silently.
- **Commas:** no serial comma: "technology, design and management". Add one only where a list would be ambiguous without it.
- **Numbers:** numerals; en dash for ranges ("3–5 owners"); "15-minute call".
- **Headings:** sentence case. H1s say the value literally, in the reader's words.
- **Punctuation:** no exclamation marks; no emoji on the site; prefer full stops, colons and commas to em dashes.
- **Bold:** sparingly, for the one point that matters, or as a short lead phrase on list items.
- **CTAs:** specific and low-pressure: "Talk it through", "Book a 15-minute call". Not "Get started today!".

## How to work

### Writing new copy
1. Pick the reader and setting, and set the dial from "How the voice flexes".
2. Draft, opening on what you do, plainly. Where you mention the reader's setup, describe or ask rather than assert.
3. Check the draft against the principles table, "Evidence" and "Facts about Winston" row by row, then reread it as a sceptical business owner. Fix anything vague, inflated or unsourced.
4. Deliver the copy. If you left placeholders or assumed a fact, say so in a line after it.

### Reviewing a draft
Return the revised copy first, then the changes that matter most, each tied to its rule ("Removed 'uniquely positioned': consultant word"). List factual problems first: invented numbers, overstated facts about Winston, workshop experience he doesn't have, unconfirmed client names. Keep the author's good lines, but replace a structure that breaks the voice rather than polishing it. If the user only wants feedback, give the list without a rewrite.
